Welcome to TSG ResearchLab

WHO WE ARE! 

We are a unique non-profit research institution dedicated to the fields of sports and exercise science.

As a subsidiary of TSG Hoffenheim, we are a team of experts in sports science, physiology, psychology, and data science.

Our mission is to connect science and sports in an interdisciplinary way, to advance practice-oriented research, and to create sustainable impulses for society, education, and technology.

WHAT WE DO!

We bridge the gap between science and sports practice.

By gaining, preparing, and sharing scientific knowledge in the context of sports and movement, we develop strategies that improve psychological and physical performance.

Our goal is to sustainably enhance performance and health through practical and forward-looking research, including the promotion of health through prevention and rehabilitation from injuries and illnesses.

How Players, Coaches and Parents Perceived a Bio-Banded Training Session

Biological maturation can strongly influence the physical and technical development of young football players. However, youth teams are usually organised according to chronological age, despite considerable differences in maturity. Bio-banding offers an alternative by grouping players according to their biological development. Our recent study examined how female youth players, coaches and parents perceived a bio-banded training session.

Study design

The study included 42 players from the U12, U14 and U15 age groups, seven coaches and academy staff members, and 30 parents. Players were allocated to four groups based on skeletal age estimations obtained using an ultrasound-based method.

The session combined technical exercises and small-sided games. Training objectives were adapted to each player’s maturity status, including developmentally appropriate challenges such as 1v1 situations over larger spaces.

Positive perceptions among players, coaches and parents

The responses to the bio-banded training session were largely positive across all groups. Ninety-three per cent of players felt comfortable in their group, 85 per cent enjoyed the session and 76 per cent believed that regular bio-banding could support their development. Most players did not feel less confident, under increased pressure or unhappy about training away from their usual teammates. At the same time, the perceived physical and technical challenge varied according to the players’ maturity status, suggesting that bio-banding can create more individualised training demands.

Coaches and parents also showed broad acceptance. Six of the seven coaches stated that they would support the use of bio-banding in regular training, while parents raised few concerns. Clear communication about the concept may have contributed to this positive response.

Practical implications

The findings provide several considerations for practitioners:

  • Use bio-banding as a complement to conventional age-group training.
  • Repeat sessions over time rather than evaluating the approach after one session.
  • Adapt training objectives to the maturity status and individual needs of each player.
  • Inform players, coaches and parents clearly before implementation.
  • Consider the perspectives of all groups when evaluating bio-banding.

Looking Ahead: Future Research on Bio-Banding

The study provides an initial insight into the feasibility and acceptance of bio-banding in female youth football. However, it involved only one training session and a small sample of coaches. Longitudinal research is therefore needed to determine whether repeated bio-banded sessions influence player development and whether positive perceptions are sustained over time.

Nevertheless, the findings support the use of bio-banding as a potential tool for creating more individualised and developmentally appropriate training environments.

Biomechanical Risk Factors for Adductor Injuries in Youth Football

Adductor-related injuries are a major concern in youth soccer, accounting for approximately 20–25% of all muscle injuries. To investigate the underlying biomechanical risk factors, we conducted a joint research project with the Institute of Biomechanics and Orthopaedics at the German Sport University Cologne.

Over a four-year prospective research period, approximately 100 elite youth soccer players from the U12 to U17 age groups participated in six measurement blocks. Standardised yet realistic instep passes were recorded in the Footbonaut. High-speed infrared cameras enabled detailed three-dimensional motion capture, complemented by isometric force measurements of the adductor muscles. Based on these data, biomechanical models were used to quantify the mechanical loading of the lower limb, including the adductors. In total, more than 10,000 passes were analysed.

The findings indicate that adductor injuries are not primarily associated with a single excessive movement. Instead, they appear to develop in the context of repeated unfavourable movement patterns over weeks and months. This highlights the importance of considering cumulative mechanical loading when investigating injury risk in youth soccer.

The analysis also demonstrated that adductor loading is influenced not only by the passing action itself, but also by the preceding approach movement towards the ball. The way in which players prepare and initiate the movement therefore represents an important aspect of the overall biomechanical load. These findings emphasise that injury-prevention strategies should address both passing technique and the preceding approach mechanics. A controlled and coordinated movement of the hip and knee during the approach should be encouraged, rather than a predominantly lateral or stiff movement pattern.

In practical terms, players should avoid approaching the ball with a largely lateral movement or a stiff swing leg. Instead, the movement should be prepared through coordinated hip and knee motion. This approach provides a basis for identifying unfavourable loading patterns at an early stage and addressing them through targeted technical training.

“Do fast players remain fast and slow players remain slow?” Long-term development of maximal sprinting speed in highly trained U12 to U19 soccer players over an 8-year period

We recently published a new study in the International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching examining the long-term development of maximal sprinting speed in highly trained male academy players from U12 to U19.Based on data from 475 players collected over an eight-year period, the study looked at how sprint performance develops during adolescence and whether players maintain their relative speed ranking over time. While absolute sprinting speed improved consistently across all age groups, especially between U14 and U16, the findings showed that relative performance was less stable than commonly assumed: many players changed performance groups over the years, and long-term stability was only moderate. These results challenge the simple idea that fast players always remain fast and slow players always remain slow. For practice, this means that current sprint performance should be interpreted with care and always viewed within the broader developmental context when making talent identification and selection decisions.

New paper: Leading through performance crises in professional soccer

Performance crises are a recurring reality in professional soccer. Coaches operate in a highly volatile environment, facing intense pressure from fans, media, and club management to deliver results. Frequent coach dismissals and constant changes are common, with significant consequences not only for coaches themselves but also for players, who must continually adapt to new leadership and expectations. These periods of underperformance can trigger psychological distress and create a self-reinforcing cycle that further intensifies the crisis.

During such challenging times, coaches play a crucial role. Their strategies and behaviors can either help teams recover or exacerbate the situation. Managing a performance crisis requires balancing external pressures, team dynamics, and individual psychological processes, all while maintaining focus on team performance and well-being.

In our published study, we explore how professional soccer coaches navigate these high-pressure moments. This research was conducted in collaboration with the Karlsruher Institut für Technologie (KIT) and their research team: Darko Jekauc, Constantin Rausch, and Julian Fritsch.

Research Fields

RESEARCH FIELDS

Helix

Using our unique Helix-Arena, we examine perception and cognitive skills like decision making in a 360-degree virtual environment in high performance athletes, healthy individuals and specific patient populations.

Cognitive Skills

Our research explores how cognitive skills develop in male and female athletes, how they relate to athletic performance, and how different assessment methods influence results. We aim to advance the understanding of talent development and the practical value of cognitive testing in elite sports.

Talent

We aim to enhance talent identification, selection, and development through a holistic approach that integrates data from multiple disciplines related to soccer performance. Using advanced statistical methods like machine learning analysis, we further aim to reliably predict future physical performance by identifying both individual and group-level development pathways.

Growth and Biological Maturation

This project investigates how individual differences in growth and maturation affect performance evaluation, talent (de-) selection, as well as physiological and psychological development in male and female youth athletes. It aims to ensure fair participation and developmental opportunities for all players.

Personality

We systematically assess personality traits in athletes to analyze their stability and variations across gender, age, playing position, and expertise level. We also examine how these traits relate to performance within a variety of sports, with a strong focus on soccer.

Speed, Strength and Endurance

Our research advances the understanding of physiological processes of the key physical abilities speed, strength, and endurance. We aim to improve both diagnostic methods and physical development, ultimately enhancing assessment practices and evidence-based training strategies.

Health and Well-Being

We create and develop tools to promote health, well-being, and performance across diverse populations - from grassroots sports to the broader health sector and the general public.

Performance Analysis

Through the use of advanced technologies and data analysis, we systematically investigate technical, tactical, physical, and psychological factors of match performance in football on both individual-player and team level.

Mental Health

Our research aims to identify symptom trajectories and risk factors for mental-health issues in athletes and coaches. We aim to foster overall psychological well-being and sustainable performance, with a special focus on vulnerable groups such as female and youth athletes.
